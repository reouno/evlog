e117: does a map at an animal's size have places and a year?

Experiment report - 2026-11-14 - rung 1 of #135: one run of twenty bare years on a 16 km map of islands, terrain seed 23

TL;DR

A 16 km map of islands across 44-64 N now has places and a year. Fronts reach the south in winter and stay north in summer: the south's lowlands get 7% of their rain in summer and their soil falls to 0.28 of its fill; the north has rain in every season and four months of snow. A lee is about twice as dry as the windward side, not five times. Nothing lives here yet. Next: plants at metres.

1. Question

#135 rebuilds the world at an animal's size; its first rung is the map. On a first look the season changed and the user could not tell what differed, so the year's water was added.

  1. Places. Latitude and the westerlies give at least 10 C north to south, a windward side five times wetter than the lee, and a snow line.
  2. A walkable ground. A 5 m ground drawn once from the coarse one reads as a landscape an animal could walk.
  3. A year that is seen (added 2026-11-14). A place looks different in January and August, and in north and south.

2. The world

128 x 128 cells of 125 m, a fifth land, latitudes 44-64 N from south to north, a year of 365 days, wind from the west-south-west. The air is followed across the map (Figure 1). Rain on freezing land lies as snow and melts by the degrees above freezing. The 5 m ground is drawn once, at the end.

Runs. One run on Ubuntu, six threads, terrain seed 23: twenty bare years, the last ten kept, 95 s. Lowlands are land under 300 m. Months run from the spring equinox, so summer is 20 June - 18 September. We read:

  • rain - mm a year, a quarter or a month.
  • soil's fill - the share of the soil's water store that is full; 1 is soaked.
  • months of snow - months whose mean pack is over 5 mm of water; 0 is no snow line.
  • west against east - an island's lowland rain on each side of its summit; equal means no lee.

3.1 Places differ by height and latitude; a lee is a strip behind a height

Height is the strongest contrast: 2,176 mm falls over 1,200 m, and the high island's rain runs from 28 to 2,734 mm. The lee is weaker than asked because it is narrow: the fronts refill air that a slope has wrung within a few km, so only lowland close behind a height stays dry. Snow lies four months on the north's lowlands, five on the summits, never in the south.

3.2 The south has a rainless summer, the north rain in every season

In winter the fronts reach every row and rain is nearly even from south to north. In summer they stay north and the south's lowlands get 36 mm in three months. The ground follows the air: the south's soil is back at 0.95 by winter, and the north's stays at 0.87 through summer. Before the fronts existed this map had no dry season, and a south wetter than its north.

3.3 The dry season has a place on the map

The dry season is a place as well as a time: in summer the southern lowlands and the lees empty, while the heights above them stay wetter. Water leaves the land the same way: the two driest months each carry 3% of the year's run-off, and on the map page 476 of 2,980 stream cells are dry beds in mid August, against 175 in January.

3.4 On the map page, the same place twice

4. Discussion

The design asked for a lee five times drier and got two. That is a matter of scale more than of tuning: these islands are a few km across and a front refills the air within a few km, so a rain shadow here is a strip behind a height, not a side of an island. Places on this map differ mostly by height, latitude and season.

The reading holds under bare ground, one terrain seed and ten years. Plants will draw the soil down further, and shade it. Years already differ: the land's rain runs from 437 to 1,045 mm over the ten.

Not built: weather on the screen (a front is a day and a half of rain in the run; the page draws mean months), snow that cools its ground, erosion, and the sun's tilt on the page. One thing in base/'s law was noticed and left: a year's warmth anomaly reaches a land cell twice, so the land's years differ by up to 1.85 times the sea's.

5. Conclusion and next step

Partly, yes, yes. The map has a dry-summer south, a snowy north, wet heights and narrow lees, and its 5 m ground reads as a landscape. Whether the year is seen on the page is the user's to say. The crate's laws go to base/; the next rung, plants at metres, copies it.
